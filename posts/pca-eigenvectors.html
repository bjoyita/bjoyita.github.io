Before we calculate PCA, we need two unfamiliar ideas: eigenvectors and eigenvalues. We can understand their meaning using a sheet, some arrows and a simple stretching rule.

What is an arrow telling us?

An arrow has a length and a direction. We can describe it by how far its tip extends horizontally and vertically from its starting point. We call this a vector. The arrow is a picture of the vector.

For example, an arrow starting at the center and ending 2 cm to the right and 2 cm above it has a horizontal part of 2 cm and a vertical part of 2 cm. Its length is not 4 cm: using Pythagoras, its length is √(2² + 2²), approximately 2.83 cm.

We stretch the sheet horizontally

Imagine a square sheet initially 10 cm wide and 10 cm high. We stretch it to 20 cm wide while its height remains 10 cm. Its center stays fixed. This is an idealized, uniform deformation: every horizontal distance from the center doubles, and every vertical distance remains unchanged. It is a geometric example, not a prediction of how an unconstrained real sheet responds to loading.

Try the stretching rule

Blue: horizontal arrow. Green: vertical arrow. Orange: diagonal arrow. All arrows start at the marked center. The sheet outlines show its boundaries. Change the factor and observe which arrows stay along their original lines.

We examine the numbers

Original arrow parts (cm)New arrow parts (cm)What happens?
Horizontal: (2, 0)(4, 0)Length doubles; direction stays horizontal.
Vertical: (0, 2)(0, 2)Length and direction stay unchanged.
Diagonal: (2, 2)(4, 2)Length changes from 2.83 to 4.47 cm; direction changes.

The diagonal arrow's angle above the horizontal changes from 45° to approximately 26.6°. The horizontal and vertical arrows remain along their original lines. This difference is what we want to notice.

What is a transformation?

A transformation is a specified rule for changing a vector. Our rule doubles the horizontal part and leaves the vertical part unchanged. We use the letter \(T\) as a short name for this rule.

If the horizontal and vertical parts are \(x\) and \(y\), measured in centimeters, the two instructions are:

\[x_{\mathrm{new}}=2x+0y,\qquad y_{\mathrm{new}}=0x+1y.\]

The zeros mean that the vertical part does not contribute to the new horizontal part, and the horizontal part does not contribute to the new vertical part.

Why write a matrix?

We arrange the coefficients of these two instructions in a rectangular table. This table is a matrix. Its first row gives the horizontal instruction. Its second row gives the vertical instruction.

\[T=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

We write a vector as a column: horizontal part first, vertical part second. Matrix multiplication applies each row's instruction to that column:

\[\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\times2+0\times2\\0\times2+1\times2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\2\end{pmatrix}.\]

The coefficients are dimensionless here. The arrow parts on both sides are in centimeters. The matrix represents the rule we already described; it does not introduce a different operation.

Now we can name eigenvectors and eigenvalues

A nonzero vector is an eigenvector of a matrix when applying that matrix produces a scalar multiple of the original vector. In our example, the horizontal arrow is multiplied by 2 and the vertical arrow by 1. Their multiplying factors are their eigenvalues.

\[T\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v.\]

Here \(\mathbf v\) is the nonzero vector, \(T\mathbf v\) means apply the matrix's rule, and \(\lambda\) is the multiplying factor. For the horizontal vector, \(\lambda=2\). For the vertical vector, \(\lambda=1\). The diagonal vector is not an eigenvector of this particular matrix when the horizontal factor is 2.

An eigenvalue can also be negative, which reverses an arrow, or zero, which maps it to zero. For a negative factor the vector remains along the same line but points the other way. If our horizontal factor is 1, nothing changes: every nonzero arrow is then an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.

Why is this useful for materials?

In continuum mechanics, a small-strain tensor can also be represented by a symmetric matrix. For example, a two-dimensional strain matrix with diagonal entries 0.02 and −0.01 and zero off-diagonal entries has horizontal and vertical eigenvector directions. Its eigenvalues are the principal strains: 2% extension and 1% contraction. These strain eigenvalues are dimensionless strains, not the stretching factors used above.

For PCA, we will use a different matrix calculated from our measurements. We will not physically stretch specimens. Its eigenvectors will define combinations of measurement features, and its eigenvalues will describe the variance along those combinations.
